ORB Research Report

2026.01.01 – 2026.08.19 · 163 trading days · real ticks · 3 sessions, 600+ configurations · FundingPips 2-Step Standard

One configurable MQL5 expert advisor, tested across three sessions, six range lengths, seven targets, and every stop-management rule worth trying. This is what survived.

Verdict

One configuration works: Asia, 00:00 UTC, 15-minute range, M1 confirmation, stop at the range midpoint, 2R target, stop moved to −0.5R once the trade reaches +0.5R, 60-minute hold cap, 2% risk. With the close-position filter at 0.25 (§12) that returns +0.404 R per trade in 2026, a 44.6% win rate and an 86.6% chance of passing both challenge phases in about 19 trading days.

Everything else failed. London loses in every configuration tested, and New York is flat at every range length — despite having the widest ranges of the three sessions.

London is dead in all four configurations — pass rates of 1.4–5.1%, and a 20.5% win rate at RR 2 means its opening range carries no directional information at all.

New York has the widest ranges of any session and no edge. 0.364% of price in 2026, best case +0.006 ± 0.114. That is the single most important negative result here: it kills the theory that range size alone produces an edge.

The likely mechanism: the New York cash open is a two-sided, news-driven spike whose opening range gets violated in both directions, so the break carries no information. Asia's 2026 breakouts ride sustained one-way flow from the gold bull market. Wide range without directional persistence is whipsaw with a bigger stop.

DST correction

The first session comparison anchored London and New York to fixed UTC hours (07:00 and 13:30) — the summer opens. In winter they open at 08:00 and 14:30 UTC, so roughly five months of every year measured an hour early: London during the pre-open, New York during US pre-market.

Re-run on each zone's own clock and DST rule, Asia was unchanged (as it must be), London went −0.137 → −0.207 and New York −0.036 → −0.082. The sloppy version had been flattering both. The EA itself was never affected — TimeZones.mqh resolves each zone through its own switch dates and TestTimeZones asserts it.

Answer: the midpoint. +0.362 for 2026 against +0.138 for the far side.

Tight stops are catastrophic, and monotonically so. A quarter of the range loses 0.272 R per trade: the stop sits inside normal noise, so it gets taken out before the move resolves and you pay full risk on a coin that never lands. Everything below 0.5 is worse in every year. This is the most robust single finding in the study.

The far side is the defensive choice — the stop is far enough away that fewer trades die on noise, giving the highest win rate of the wide settings. It earns under half as much, and sizing 2% against a full-range stop means much smaller positions.

Answer: trigger at +0.5R, move to −0.5R. Never move to breakeven.

Breakeven is the single most destructive setting in the study. At a +0.25R trigger it turns +0.387 R into −0.234 R — it flips a profitable configuration into a losing one. A breakeven stop sits exactly where price routinely retests after a breakout: you get stopped for nothing on trades that would have worked, and you keep every full loss.

Early triggers are worse than late ones at every destination — moving early tightens the stop while the trade is still inside noise.

On pure EV, not moving at all wins (+0.387, and +0.338 on real ticks). The move costs about 0.005–0.02 R and buys roughly 5 points of pass rate (81.4% against 75.9%). Keep it.

Bigger range does not mean better. Asia's median range goes 1108 → 1502 → 2040 points while EV goes +0.333 → +0.007 → +0.158. Same lesson New York taught in §02.

Wider ranges mean wider stops, so each trade is a smaller share of the account and the equity curve is calmer. On Asia that smoothness costs more expectancy than it buys in pass rate.

New York fails at every range length — −0.015, −0.114, −0.056. Its 60-minute range is 3020 points, nearly three times Asia's 15-minute range, and it still loses.

Honest caveat: the 30m/M3 result being the worst of three rather than the middle is probably noise. The whole spread is about 1.5 standard errors wide, so treat the ranking as directional.

This is the central lesson, and it disagrees with every EV table above.

The highest-EV configuration ranks eleventh. Turning the stop move off raises EV from +0.362 to +0.387 and lowers pass rate from 84.1% to 80.7%. The reason is the sd(R) column: 1.37 against 1.49.

A prop challenge is a race between +8% and −10%, not a long-run expectancy game. Variance reduction is worth more than the expectancy it costs, because the drawdown barrier can end you before the edge has had time to express itself.

But this cuts the other way too. Along the RR dial you buy win rate by giving up edge, which is a bad trade: RR 1 gives 47.8% win rate and 79.0% pass, against RR 2's 30.8% and 86.4%. Higher win rate helps only when it comes free — from better entries, not from a nearer target.

Speed is nearly free. Pass rate barely moves while median time drops from 19 days to 7 — because pass probability is decided by the sequence of trades, not the calendar. Trades per day only changes how fast you consume that sequence. The one real cost is the 5% daily limit, which starts biting at three trades a day.

Optimal risk depends on whether you have an edge

Risk / tradeEdge exists (+0.24R)No edge (−0.02R)

The optimum inverts. With an edge, risk small — big bets add ruin chances you do not need. Without one, risk big: small bets let a tiny negative drift grind you from 0% to −10% over hundreds of trades and you never get a run to +8%. Variance is your friend only when you have nothing else.

Rotating accounts does not help

Rotating one account per day does not change any account's pass probability — each still needs the same trade sequence, and you have only slowed the calendar by N×. Expected passes = N × P(pass) either way.

What rotation buys is decorrelation. Trade all N accounts the same day on the same signal and they all pass or all fail together: same average, enormous variance. But there is a better way to get independence without the calendar penalty — decorrelate by session or symbol, not by day. Same date, one trade each, uncorrelated outcomes. That was the original argument for testing London and New York; §02 shows neither qualifies, so on gold this remains unsolved.

Monitoring rule

The 15-minute range must be at least 0.15% of the gold price. At gold 4 500 that is about 675 points ($6.75). Below 0.10% the strategy clearly loses. Check the median of the last 20 sessions monthly; if it falls under the line, switch the filter on or stop trading Asia.

Necessary, not sufficient — New York clears this line in all three years and still has no edge (§02). Treat it as a kill switch for Asia, not a search tool for new markets.

12The close-position filter

Rule: at the moment the range closes, score how near price was to the side it then breaks. Skip the trade if that score is too low.

The score, and what it means
ScoreWhere the final range bar closedWhat the break is

Same failure mode that makes New York unusable (§02): a range violated in both directions carries no information. Rejection ends the day rather than waiting for a break the other way — deliberate, since the opposite break scores 1 − score by construction, so allowing it would turn every rejection into a coin flip on the other side.

⚠ This filter is a regime bet

Tested on a choppy market instead of a trending one, the effect inverts: below-25% becomes the best quadrant and above-75% turns negative.

That is mechanism, not noise. In a choppy market the range mean-reverts, so a break away from where price sat pays. In a trending market the range is a pause in a move, so a break continuing it pays.

Consequence: if the kill switch in §11 fires, this filter should be turned off or inverted, not merely tolerated.

Everything below 0.25 is genuinely bad; everything above it is profitable. Seven trades out of 119 account for the entire benefit, and five of them average −0.938 R.

Past 0.25 the pass rate keeps climbing — 86.6% to 92.7% — but only because you are taking fewer trades. That is buying a smoother ride by deleting winners, and paying seven extra calendar days for it. Fees per pass improve by 0.07 across that whole stretch, which does not cover it.

Chosen — 0.25

It removes the entire tail and nothing else.

EV +0.333 → +0.404. Win rate 42.0% → 44.6%. Pass rate 81.6% → 86.6%. Attempts per pass 1.23 → 1.15. Cost: one extra day and 7% of setups.

Caveats

In a choppy market it costs you instead of helping — the effect inverts, as the note above sets out. A directional regime bet, not a law.

It was one of five candidates. Break strength, trend alignment and range efficiency were rejected for non-monotone buckets or effects that failed to hold in both samples. A volume filter did survive (+0.391 against +0.080 for breaks on under 0.8× the range's median tick count) but was dropped as impractical to judge in real time. Testing five filters across ~20 buckets guarantees one looks good by chance; what earns this one its place is that the direction holds in both samples and the mechanism states in a sentence.

Simulator agreement: predicted 93% kept, +0.436 EV, 47.3% WR, 89.1% pass at 0.25. MT5 returned 93%, +0.404, 44.6%, 86.6% — optimistic by the usual ~0.03 R.

Why this one is believable: there is a mechanism, not just a number. Friday's Asia session is the last of the week — by the time London opens, position-squaring into the weekend dominates. Why Tuesday stays. Tuesday is the second-weakest day and cutting it also improves every headline number. Caveat

24 Friday trades at ±0.240, so the true value sits somewhere between −0.72 and +0.24. Five days were tested, so the worst of five landing near two standard errors out is partly what chance produces. What tips it: negative expectancy, a 20.8% win rate, a mechanism, and simultaneous improvement in both speed and pass rate. That combination is rare enough to act on.

14Rules of thumb

  1. Only Asia works. London is negative in every configuration tested; New York is flat at every range length.
  2. Do not trade Friday. The only losing day of the week, at a 20.8% win rate. Dropping it improves expectancy, win rate, pass rate and speed at once — the rare change with no trade-off.
  3. Shortest range, fastest candle. 15 minutes with M1 beats 30/M3 and 60/M5. The edge lives in the first 15 minutes and the earliest break; lengthening the range dilutes it.
  4. Never tighten the stop inside noise. A quarter-range stop loses 0.27 R per trade. A breakeven stop-move flips a profitable configuration into a losing one. Both fail for the same reason.
  5. RR 2, never RR 1. Worth about five times the expectancy. The higher win rate at RR 1 is a trap — you pay for it in edge.
  6. A late breakout is a bad breakout. Breaks after minute 15 drag EV from +0.375 to +0.181. Stop looking.
  7. Optimise pass rate, not profit factor. They select different parameters. The highest-EV setup ranks eleventh on pass rate.
  8. Win rate is worth paying for — but only if it is free. At constant edge, 30% → 40% win rate is worth 10 points of pass rate. Buying it by shortening the target is not.
  9. Range size is necessary, not sufficient. New York has the widest ranges on the board and no edge.
  10. Speed is nearly free; risk sizing is not. More trades per day barely dents pass rate. Changing risk per trade moves it 15–30 points.
  11. Skip reversal breaks. If the range closed at the far end from the side that broke, price flipped across the whole range first. Five such trades in 119 averaged −0.94 R. Cutting the bottom quarter is nearly free.
  12. Trust nothing inside ±0.05 R. That is the simulator's error bar against MT5. Several "improvements" in this study were noise, and saying so is cheaper than trading them.

15Method and caveats

The Strategy Tester needs about 90 seconds per real-tick pass over 2.6 years, so 600 configurations would take hours. Instead BarDump.mq5 recorded 730 857 M1 bars with tick counts across broker hours 01:00–18:59, and the Python harness in research/ replays them. Entry timing depends only on the range break, so stop distance, target and stop-management rules can all be re-evaluated against identical recorded paths. Shortlists were then confirmed in MT5 on real ticks — every table labelled "real ticks" is a genuine backtest.

Known gaps, all of which flatter the simulation:

  • M1 OHLC rather than real ticks. Intrabar order is unknown, so when one bar contains both stop and target the simulation assumes the stop hit first. Real ticks still take out stops it misses — MT5 recorded 7 hold-cap exits where the sim predicted 5.
  • Spread is a per-year median (21 / 28 / 52 points), measured at the Asia open, applied to every session. The Asia open is thin so the median understates; New York liquidity is deeper so its result is flattered.
  • Commission modelled flat at $3.04 per lot per side.
  • 481 setups against MT5's 455 — about 5% of days differ, most likely history gaps and entry-window boundary handling.

Treat absolute levels as ±0.05 R. Rankings are reliable; levels are not. And 119 trades in 2026 on one symbol and one session is enough to rank parameters, nowhere near enough to establish an edge.

16Roadmap

  • US100.cash on its own cash open. The only untested idea left with a real rationale: an index's cash open is its primary session, not a mid-session news spike, which is structurally different from gold at 13:30 UTC. Cost structure needs re-measuring, not assuming.
  • Pre-commit the acceptance threshold before sweeping any new session or symbol — say 2026 EV above +0.20 R with win rate above 40% — and trade every stream that clears it rather than ranking and picking the best. Selecting a maximum from several noisy 120-trade estimates is how a filter that works on paper fails live.
  • Verify BrokerFollowsUSDST by running CheckBrokerOffset. August is DST under both rulesets so the current setting is inferred, and it matters for the week each autumn when US and EU dates disagree.
  • Watch the range, not the backtest. The kill switch in §11 is the one piece of ongoing monitoring this strategy needs.
